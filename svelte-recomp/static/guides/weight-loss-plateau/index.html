Weight loss plateau: really stuck, or just water?

A Slim Chance guide · November 2026

The scale hasn't moved in a week, maybe two, and you haven't changed a thing. Most of the time that isn't a plateau at all. Here is how to tell, why weight loss slows on its own, and what actually helps when it really has stopped.

The short version. Daily weight swings by half a kilo or more, so a week of flat readings often hides a real downward trend. Look at the trend over two to three weeks of mornings before deciding anything. If it really is flat, the usual reasons are eating has crept up, moving less, or simply being lighter than when you started. Metabolism "shutting down" is rarely the answer.

First, is it a plateau?

A kilo of body fat holds about 7,000 kcal. A steady 500 kcal a day deficit takes off roughly half a kilo a week, while water, salt, carbohydrate and the timing of your last meal move the scale by half a kilo to two kilos from one morning to the next. So a real loss of 0.5 kg a week can easily look flat for seven or ten days.

The way through is the trend, not the reading: weigh most mornings under the same conditions, keep every number, high ones included, and look at the line through them. Two to three weeks of mornings is usually enough to see whether that line is really flat. None of these is fat. They pass, and when they do the scale often drops in one go: the "whoosh" people describe. Our guide why the scale went up overnight covers each cause in more detail.

Weight loss slows down on its own

A smaller body burns less. A widely used model of human weight change (Hall and colleagues, The Lancet, 2011) found that for an average adult with overweight, each lasting change of about 24 kcal a day in intake moves body weight by roughly a kilo in the end, half of it within the first year. The same model shows why the old "3,500 calories per pound" rule overpromises: as you lose weight, the deficit you started with shrinks, so the same eating loses less each month. Slowing down is expected, and it doesn't mean anything is broken.

Is my metabolism broken?

Almost certainly not. Bodies do burn somewhat less than their new size alone would predict after weight loss, an effect called adaptive thermogenesis. In controlled studies it is usually modest, often under about 100 kcal a day, and larger only after very big or very fast losses. It can turn a steady loss into a slow one; it rarely turns it into none.

The usual real reasons

  • Eating has crept up. Portions grow, extras go unnoticed. That isn't a character flaw: studies that measure intake objectively find most people underestimate what they eat, sometimes by a lot. You don't need a perfect log to fix it. A consistent one is enough, because a budget learned from your own scale absorbs what you don't write down (why consistent beats accurate).
  • Moving less. Fewer steps on busy or tired weeks is common. Walking costs roughly 0.5 to 0.8 kcal per kg of body weight per km, so 3,000 fewer steps a day at 100 kg is about 110 to 180 kcal a day. A watch or phone shows it plainly.
  • Short sleep. In lab studies, short nights raised next-day eating by about 385 kcal on average (a meta-analysis of 11 studies, 172 people), and sleeping about an hour longer cut intake by about 270 kcal a day in a four-week trial of 80 adults.
  • You're lighter now. The deficit that worked at the start is smaller at your new weight (see above).

What helps, once it really is flat

  1. Confirm it on the trend, over two to three weeks of mornings, not a few readings.
  2. Check steps and sleep before cutting food: they're often the quiet change.
  3. Keep protein up and lift something twice a week: it protects muscle while you lose. Very large deficits work against this; a meta-analysis found deficits above about 500 kcal a day blunted muscle gains from training.
  4. Then change one thing, a little: about 100 to 200 kcal a day less, or a daily walk, and give it two to three weeks. Big cuts invite hunger and rebound.
  5. Never chase a number with a crash diet. If you're losing more than about 1% of your weight a week, or the plateau comes with feeling unwell, talk to a doctor.
